This is something Ashok spends a lot of time on. He’s formally trained in it. I’ll give you a five-minute overview of what may be holding you back. It’s the theory of constraints, which comes from Goldratt, an Israeli researcher who applied it to manufacturing, though it works beautifully for small businesses too. His most famous book is The Goal.
Let me give you a very quick summary of his life’s work. His central idea is that every system has one weakest link. If you look at this crowded street, bringing in a Lamborghini won’t get you to the other end faster. The speed of the system is defined not by your own capability but by whatever is slowing the system down. If you want to move fast, you must understand what is holding you back and remove that bottleneck.
Many other management theories talk about studying everything, building balanced scorecards, doing exhaustive reviews. But in a 10 or 15 crore company, management bandwidth is limited, and all of that can be a distraction. The simplest, most effective approach is this: gather your management team in one room and ask, “What is the one thing that is holding us back from growing 10x.” One thing, not ten.
If you find the constraint, that constraint is also your lever. Fix it, and your growth accelerates. If removing that one constraint can release a 10x increase in throughput, you’ve found the right one.
Internal constraints and external constraints both exist. External constraints are things like “the market isn’t big enough.” Most of you do not have external constraints. Most constraints in companies of your size are internal, which is good because you can fix them yourself.
Internal constraints include things like: “We only hire this type of person,” or “Every CEO must approve every communication,” or “Only I can decide pricing,” or “Every project must have a fixed margin,” or “I won’t hire someone who earns more than me.” Many such rules silently limit your growth.
Often founders don’t realize these constraints exist.
A powerful example is the idea of a policy constraint, illustrated by Bhishma in the Mahabharata. He was the most powerful warrior, fully capable, but had bound himself with vows. “I will not be king. I will not fight for the Pandavas. I will not do this. I will not do that.” His own self-imposed constraints prevented everything he was capable of. Many founders do this unconsciously in their own businesses.
Identifying those constraints takes work. Spend time with your team and ask: “If I found this constraint and strengthened it, would the whole business gain momentum.”
Someone may say, “Our constraint is salespeople.” So hire salespeople. Then you ask, “Why two? Why not twenty.” If twenty won’t help, maybe the constraint isn’t salespeople, it’s that you don’t know what to sell. That becomes the constraint.
If adding to the constraint immediately increases throughput, that’s the right constraint.
Here is the basic process Goldratt suggests:
1. Identify the constraint.

2. Squeeze the most out of the constraint.
Make sure constraint resources are not doing anything except the work that moves the needle.
For example, if sales is the constraint, your sales team should not be spending time filling forms or driving visitors around.

3. Subordinate everything else to support the constraint.
Rearrange people, slow down fast movers to support slow movers, provide whatever the constraint needs.

4. Elevate the capacity of the constraint.
Bring in more resources, hire, change structure, do whatever expands it.

5. When the constraint shifts, circle back and repeat.

It’s simple but very hard to do because it forces you to admit that only one thing matters at a time.

The reality is that it helps to be a leader. Nobody wants to work with someone who isn’t a leader. When you have a choice, you always choose the leader. When you go to a store, you pick the leading brands. Leadership matters.
There’s a well-known concept called the rule of three from Raj Sisodia and Jagdish Sheth. They studied large industries like airlines, telecom, and cloud. Their conclusion was that most industries stabilize with two or three major players, and those players take most of the market. The rest fight for the scraps.
If you imagine the market as a lion and the hyenas, the lion gets the meat, and the hyenas get the leftovers. It’s a brutal analogy, but it reflects reality. If you are running a small business and you aren’t a leader, you are waiting for leftovers. You don’t get the real opportunities. To get the real opportunities, you must be the lion, which means you must be the leader.
So what does it mean to be a leader. To be a leader in any market, you need at least 20 percent market share. This is my simplified rule. If you have 20 percent, you’re definitely in the top five and usually in the top three. If three companies have 20 to 40 percent each, that is already 70 or 80 percent of the market. The rest—hundreds of companies—fight for the remaining 20 percent.
This is true in IT services as well. The large companies make the money. The question is, where is the room for the rest.
Here is the key idea, and if you remember only one slide today, this is the one. Let’s say you run a 10 crore business and want to grow to 20 crores. To be a leader, your market must be around 100 crores. Not a billion-dollar market. Not “the entire IT industry.” In a billion-dollar market, a 20 crore company is invisible. Nobody knows you. Nobody comes to you. You can’t build a reputation or leadership position.
Define your market such that the total market size is about five times your aspirational size. If you want to be 20 crores, define a 100 crore market. If you want to be 100 crores, define a 500 crore market. That is how you become a leader.

Another important idea: are you selling elephants or rabbits. Think in terms of deal size.
High price multiplied by low volume is the elephant strategy.
Low price multiplied by high volume is the rabbit strategy.
A twenty-crore company selling five-crore deals needs only a few wins a year. If your deals are fifty thousand rupees, you need hundreds. You cannot run both strategies at once.
